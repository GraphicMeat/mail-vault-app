How to Leave an Email Provider Without Losing Years of Messages

3 min read

Changing email providers usually starts with a new address or a new subscription. The difficult part is everything accumulated in the old account: invoices, conversations, attachments, and the address other people still use to reach you.

A successful move has two outcomes. New mail reaches the right place, and old mail remains readable after access to the original provider ends. Test those outcomes separately. A migration progress bar can finish while an overlooked folder or sending identity still needs attention.

1. Inventory more than the inbox

List every account and alias, then inspect Inbox, Sent, Archive, Drafts, and custom folders. Include messages from any additional mailboxes you are authorized to move. Note filters, signatures, contacts, forwarding rules, and scheduled messages; exporting email does not necessarily export those settings.

If the move is prompted by Gmail’s third-party connection changes, first read what ends in January 2027. Replacing a connection to another account may be enough. You do not necessarily need to move the underlying mailbox or change your address.

2. Create a copy independent of the old account

Use an export, backup, or archive operation that saves complete messages. Headers and search indexes alone do not preserve message bodies and attachments. Keep the account open while the operation runs, and review any failed or skipped items before proceeding.

Save the archive in a format you can open with another tool. Individual .eml files preserve messages as files; MBOX groups messages into mailbox files. Retain the original export while experimenting with imports so that a mistake in the new account does not become your only remaining copy.

3. Verify the archive before trusting it

Open a sample that spans the entire history: an old message, a recent message, a sent message, one from a custom folder, and several with attachments. Check dates, senders, body text, and attachment contents. Disconnect from the network and repeat the checks on the saved copy.

Compare folder coverage and counts, but investigate differences instead of expecting every number to match automatically. Providers can represent the same message in multiple views or labels. A total alone cannot tell you whether the right content was preserved.

Keep a second copy on another drive or backup destination. A local archive protects you from losing access to the provider; a separate backup protects against losing that local disk.

4. Test the new account in both directions

Send a message to an independent address, reply to it, and check the result in both accounts. Test each alias you intend to keep. Confirm the displayed sender, reply address, signature, and Sent folder behavior.

If you own a domain, follow the new provider’s current setup instructions for mail routing and authentication. Plan that change separately from importing history. Keep a record of the previous configuration and verify actual delivery before relying on the new route.

5. Move the address people and services rely on

Update account recovery addresses, billing contacts, newsletters you want to keep, and important personal or business contacts. A working inbox is little help if password resets still go to an address you are about to close.

Keep the old account available during an overlap period appropriate to your situation. Review incoming mail there for forgotten dependencies. If forwarding is available, test it rather than assuming it works, and remember that it does not preserve the historical mailbox.

6. Close the old account only after the checks pass

Use a written checklist: history verified, attachments readable, second copy created, new sending and receiving tested, recovery addresses updated, and remaining dependencies resolved. Check the provider’s current closure and recovery rules before taking the final step.
