Gmail Storage Full? Here's How to Fix It

6 min read

Gmail gives every free account 15 GB, shared across Gmail, Google Drive, and Google Photos. When you hit that limit, two things happen: you stop receiving new email (senders get a bounce message), and Google starts nagging you to buy more storage. Neither is dangerous, but both are annoying, and the fix most people reach for — paying Google $1.99/month for 100 GB — only delays the problem. Your mailbox keeps growing, and eventually you are back here again.

Here is what actually works, in order of effort.

1. Find out what's actually using your space

Before deleting anything, see where your 15 GB went. In Gmail, search has:attachment larger:10M to find big attachments, or larger:25M for anything huge. You can also check one.google.com/storage for a breakdown by Gmail, Drive, and Photos — sometimes the culprit isn't email at all. Old Google Photos backups and forgotten Drive files routinely eat more space than a decade of email text ever could.

2. Empty Trash and Spam

Deleted email in Gmail doesn't actually free space until it's purged from Trash, which happens automatically after 30 days — or immediately if you empty it yourself. Open Trash and Spam from the left sidebar and click "Empty Trash now" / "Delete all spam messages now." This alone can recover a surprising amount if you've been deleting things and assuming they were gone.

3. Delete the actual space hogs

Sort by size using the searches above, and go through the largest attachments first. Newsletters with embedded images, old resumes with photos, video files someone emailed you years ago — these are usually the biggest wins. Deleting a hundred old text-only emails barely moves the needle; deleting twenty old attachments often does.

4. Pay Google for more storage

If you need speed and don't mind a subscription, Google One starts at $1.99/month for 100 GB. It's the path of least resistance, but it's also the only option on this list that doesn't actually solve anything — it just raises the ceiling. Your mailbox will keep growing at the same rate it always has, and in a few years you'll be looking at the 2 TB tier instead.

5. Archive locally, then delete from Gmail (the permanent fix)

The workflow that actually breaks the cycle: download the emails you want to keep to your own computer, verify they're all there, then delete them from Gmail's servers. Google's storage frees up, and you still have every email — searchable, on your own disk, with no monthly fee attached to keeping them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will deleting emails from Gmail free up space immediately?

Deleting a message moves it to Trash, where it still counts against your quota for up to 30 days unless you empty Trash manually. To reclaim space right away, delete the message and then empty Trash and Spam.

Does archiving in Gmail free up storage?

No. Gmail's Archive button only removes the Inbox label — the email still lives in All Mail and still counts against your 15 GB. To actually free up server space you need to delete the message, or move it off Google's servers entirely with a local backup.

What's the fastest way to see what's using my Gmail storage?

Search larger:10M or larger:25M in Gmail to surface your biggest emails and attachments, or check the storage breakdown at one.google.com/storage to see how much is Gmail versus Drive and Photos.

Is it safe to delete old emails after archiving them with MailVault?

Yes, as long as you verify the local archive first. Open a handful of archived emails in MailVault, confirm attachments are intact, and search for a specific message you remember before deleting anything from Gmail's servers.
